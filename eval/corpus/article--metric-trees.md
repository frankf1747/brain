# Metric trees: how I stopped arguing about dashboards

By Hannah Leclerc

I have built metric trees at three companies over 12 years, and every time the reason was the same: leadership meetings had turned into arguments about whose dashboard was right. Sales had one revenue number, finance another, product a third, and each was defensible. A metric tree does not settle those arguments by decree; it makes the relationships between numbers explicit, so the disagreement moves to the place where it actually lives.

Start from one north-star metric, the number that best captures the value customers get and the business captures. Then break it into its inputs: revenue becomes customers times revenue per customer, customers become new plus retained, and so on. Each split should be arithmetic or close to it, so that a change at the top can be traced to a change somewhere below. Keep the tree no more than four levels deep. Beyond that, the lower nodes are usually activities rather than metrics, and the tree becomes a to-do list that nobody can read in a meeting.

The part that makes it work is boring: every node gets one owner and one written definition. The owner is the person who answers when the number moves. The definition says exactly which table, which filters and which time window, and it lives next to the tree, not in someone's head. In my experience the first month of building a tree is mostly spent writing those definitions, and that month is where most of the value is.
